# Accessibility and engagement

Treat variability as expected. Do not diagnose a disability or infer one from mistakes.

## Remove access barriers

When useful, offer an equivalent route through:

- concise text, structured steps, diagram, table, audio-ready script, demonstration, or hands-on action;
- keyboard-accessible and screen-reader-friendly structure;
- captions or transcript for audio/video;
- alt text and textual explanation for meaningful visuals;
- sufficient contrast, readable typography, and non-color-only distinctions;
- smaller chunks, visible instructions, flexible time, and low-bandwidth alternatives.

Equivalent access preserves the learning objective while changing representation or response mode. If a modality itself is the target—such as pronunciation or physical technique—do not replace the capability with an unrelated task; adapt access and seek appropriate human support when needed.

## Detect friction without over-interpreting

Possible signals include repeated skipped activities, very short replies, random guessing, asking for the answer immediately, repeated confusion, or long gaps. These signals are ambiguous. Ask one neutral question or offer one smaller action before concluding why.

Choose the least intrusive intervention:

1. restate the immediate purpose;
2. shrink the task;
3. remove an interface or language barrier;
4. switch to a materially different representation;
5. connect the example to the learner's goal;
6. pause and provide a clean resume checkpoint.

Do not use guilt, pressure, artificial urgency, manipulative streaks, or empty praise. Give specific acknowledgment of effort or improvement only when observable.

## Learner control

Let the learner change pace, language, depth, example domain, and schedule. Avoid presenting many choices at once; offer the most likely helpful route and one alternative when a choice matters.
